The Mapping Accessibility Project (MAP) is based at Western Washington University (WWU) in Bellingham, Washington. We collect accessibility information nobody has gathered before, and turn it into maps, reports and data that anyone can use.

## Why maps?

Disability is shaped by the places we move through, not only by our bodies. A step with no ramp, a door with no button or a room that is too loud can shut people out. This is often called the social model of disability.

Accessible maps help people plan how to get around and take part in campus life. But even a campus that meets every legal requirement can be hard to navigate. That is why we map more than ramps and elevators: trail surfaces, sound, emergency equipment, snow routes and more.

## How MAP works

1. MAP members meet regularly to talk about what people need to know and what information is missing.
2. Those gaps become student projects. Most are final projects in ENVS 421 (GIS IV: Advanced GIS Applications), where a team works on a MAP question for about ten weeks. Others are senior projects, independent studies and internships.
3. Students collect data in the field, analyze it, and share a report, a story map and a dataset. Each team builds on the work of the teams before it.
4. We share results with campus offices, community partners and the public, and choose the next questions together.

Students are learning as they go, so their results are a starting point. They show where barriers are; they are not official compliance audits.

## Our story so far

### 2023

ENVS 421 students mapped every trail in Sehome Hill Arboretum and found that only one, the Huntoon Trail, might be accessible. In the fall, student interns began writing directions from accessible parking lots and bus stops to building entrances.

### 2024

Two ENVS 421 classes mapped the Huntoon Trail in close detail and found that even this paved trail is in rough shape. An intern wrote guidelines for describing maps in plain text for screen-reader users.

### 2025

Students mapped beyond the arboretum for the first time: building audits, snow routes, fire evacuation from residence halls, and where to find defibrillators and naloxone. In the arboretum, one team resurveyed the Huntoon Trail and another recorded what it sounds like.

### 2026

ENVS 421 teams mapped automatic doors across campus, audited Arntzen Hall room by room, measured sound in Wilson Library, and worked with the Max Higbee Center on downtown Bellingham sidewalks.
